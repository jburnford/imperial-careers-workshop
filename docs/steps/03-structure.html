Structure

Minutes 12–22. What is a record here? The model proposes and fills the fields; you decide what counts as an event.

The schema

A biography becomes one JSON object: name, birth year, education string, honours with award years, a list of career events, and how the career ended. Each event has a position, a place as printed, start and end years, an acting flag and a civil or military tag. The agent and the historian settled the schema on a sample of entries before any batch ran.

The prompt

The system prompt the pipeline sends with every entry (full text). The rules are the historian’s decisions, written down:

Extract ONLY what is printed; never invent or infer facts. events: one object per distinct posting. EXPLODE compound/concurrent clauses into separate events (e.g. ‘reg.-gen., 1887, and M.L.C., 1889’ = two events). place: the colony, territory, OR locality named in the clause, AS PRINTED. If the clause names no place, carry over the previous clause’s place and set place_inherited=true; do NOT guess or upgrade a locality to its parent colony. birth_year: only if explicitly printed (‘B. 1872’); else null. NEVER infer from the earliest career date. honours: decorations/orders with their AWARD year if printed in parentheses; these award-years are NOT career events.

Every one of those rules exists because an earlier run broke it.

What came back

Trimen’s 1889 entry, structured (saved):

position place years
lecturer on botany London 1867–1875
senior assistant to department of botany British Museum 1869–1879 overlaps the first: two concurrent posts, two events
director Royal Botanical Gardens Ceylon 1880–

Plus education: "M.B. (Lond.)", two honours without years, no birth year, no terminal event. In the published graph the first and third events are placed (London Q84, Ceylon Q918153); “British Museum” is an institution, not a place, and stays unplaced.

Two kinds of model

The reference set was structured in session by a frontier model acting as the coding agent: about 200 entries, each checked. The corpus was structured by Qwen on a shared university GPU inside the script the agent wrote, at a cost the frontier model could not match for 100,000 entries. The runbook for the loop is saved. Every record says which path produced it.

The audit

Every tenth structured record was read against its source by a reviewer model and the findings were categorised (catalogue, reviewed pairs). Of the first 186 sampled:

Fully clean 129 (69%)
Records with a finding 56
Findings judged major 8 (about 3% of records)

Three of the findings, with the source beside the record (saved):

The ditto that was not resolved. Balmer’s last clause, “senr. dep. P.M.G., do., 1935”, came back with place: "do.". The rule says inherit; the model copied the abbreviation.

The appointment date that became a birth year. “BLAKELY, DONALD QUENTIN.—Clk., atty.-gen.’s off., B. Honduras, Apl., 1898.” came back with birth_year: 1898 and an event with no year. The prompt forbids exactly this. The same record expanded “off.” to “officer” instead of “office”.

The knighthood split in two. “KT. BACH. (1920)” came back as two honours, KT and BACH (1920). Three records in the sample did the same.

Why the fix was not a bigger model

The errors are not random. They are a handful of systematic patterns: a short list of abbreviations mis-expanded the same way every time (“off.”, “col.”, “govt.”, “offr. in ch.”), ditto marks left literal, one compound honour split, “mentioned in despatches” dropped. Each has a deterministic fix: an expansion table, a forward-fill pass, a merge rule, a sweep of the source for a phrase. The fixes run after the model and cost nothing per record. A frontier model on every entry would have cost more and would still have needed the audit.

You decide: what is an event. Whether an acting appointment is a posting. Whether “called to the bar, 1879” belongs in the career. Whether “leader of leg. coun. from 1906 to 1911” is a position or a description. The prompt records those decisions; the audit checks whether the model kept them.
