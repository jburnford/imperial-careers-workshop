1. The question

Minutes 0–5. Establish the research payoff and the task.

Start from a structured collection

The Imperial Careers project turned the Colonial Office List into a graph of people and career events. Here is what it holds for Henry Trimen, taken from the project’s stage-3 graph (person kgp_col1889-p554b20, attested in seven editions, 1883–1896):

Seq Position (as printed) Place Years
0 lecturer on botany London (St. Mary’s Hospital) 1867–1875
1 senior assistant to department of botany British Museum 1869–1879
2 director Royal Botanical Gardens Ceylon 1880–

The 1889 entry reads:

TRIMEN, HENRY, M.B. (Lond.), F.R.S., F.L.S. —Formerly lecturer on botany at St. Mary’s Hospital, London, 1867 to 1875; was senior assistant to department of botany, British Museum, 1869 to 1879; director Royal Botanical Gardens, Ceylon, Feb., 1880; author of numerous works and papers on pure and applied botany.

All seven editions are in the source pack. Notice what the List gives us: a name, a sequence of posts with dates and places, and a stable identity across editions. Notice what it does not give us: anything about what Trimen did, argued, or whom he worked with.

Take the question to an unstructured collection

The Tropical Agriculturist (Colombo, from 1881) printed reports, letters and reprints for the planting community. The Tropical Agriculturist project has OCR text for about 53,500 articles. A search for articles mentioning both “Trimen” and “Howard” returns 72 of them. That is a discovery set, not 72 relationships.

Our question for the hour:

Who is Henry Trimen connected to in The Tropical Agriculturist, and what evidence supports those connections?

We will work with a handful of items from 1883, when the journal ran a dispute it headlined “The Wars of the Quinologists”:

Id Item Why it is here
S02 “The Wars of the Quinologists”, July 1883, pp. 27–28 The editor frames a dispute among Howard, Trimen and Moens
S03 “Dr. Trimen’s Ledgeriana: Mr. T. N. Christie to the Rescue”, July 1883, p. 37 A planter traces the illustrated tree’s seed and nursery
S04 “Dr. Trimen’s Typical Ledgeriana Tree”, July 1883, p. 66 Walter Agar’s 234-word letter. The exercise passage.
S05, S06 “The Cinchona Calisaya (or Ledgeriana) Discussion” and “The Botany of Cinchona Ledgeriana”, December 1883, pp. 453–455 Trimen’s reply, signed from Peradeniya, with the competing identifications
S01 “Mr. Eliot Howard Extinguishing C. Pubescentis How.”, July 1881, pp. 114–115 Optional: Howard rejects a species name credited to him

We begin after OCR. Other sessions cover OCR production. The page images stay one click away, and you will need them.

Ten years ago

The problems in this hour are not new. Trading Consequences (2012–2013) text-mined 10.5 million scanned pages of nineteenth-century British sources for commodities, places and dates: historians at Saskatchewan and York, computational linguists at Edinburgh, a database team at EDINA, visualisation at St Andrews, funded by Digging into Data. The write-up is Clifford, Alex, Coates, Klein and Watson, “Geoparsing history: Locating commodities in ten million pages of nineteenth-century sources”, Historical Methods 49:3 (2016), 115–131, doi:10.1080/01615440.2015.1116419. Its difficulties are the same three we will work on, at a scale and cost that is worth remembering:

Trading Consequences, 2013 This hour
Cleaning OCR turned “lime” into “time” and “line”, long-s “salt” into “fait”; tables collapsed into 210-word “sentences” that linked “glass, glue and gelatine” to every country in a trade return. Fixes were hand-built: a hyphenation joiner, an f-to-s lexicon, a filter for table-heavy documents. 14.9% of locations in the gold standard contained OCR errors. Two sentences of a 234-word letter are wrong in the OCR, in ways that reverse the meaning. The model flags them; the page settles them (case R3).
Structuring A six-stage pipeline (format conversion, OCR post-correction, linguistic pre-processing, named-entity recognition, attribute recognition, relation extraction), adapted from the Edinburgh Geoparser by computational linguists. A relation was “same sentence”. A commodity lexicon had to be bootstrapped from customs records and Wikipedia because no expert list existed. The coding agent proposes the fields, writes the script and reports failures in minutes (step 2). The historian still decides what a record is.
Grounding GeoNames string matching with geographic coherence scoring. “Saharunpore” was not found because the gazetteer only knew “Saharanpur”; “British West India Islands” grounded to West India; a bug placed every “Italy” in a Texan town. Final F-scores: 0.62 for commodities, 0.61 for locations. Two trained annotators agreed on what counted as a commodity only 72% of the time. Semantic search over Wikidata plus statement reading, cached with timestamps (step 3). Walter Agar still is not there (case R4). Entities absent from the authority, and plausible wrong matches, are the same problem with a better search box.
Review Historians spotted false positives through the visualisations (“Saumur” as a place in a wine list); false negatives were invisible. A gold standard of double-annotated documents measured precision and recall. Same two lessons: errors you can see get fixed, errors you cannot see need a sample; and a corrected error should become a rule (step 5).

What changed is who writes the pipeline and how fast the loop from error to fix runs. What did not change is where historical judgment is needed. The paper’s closing advice to historians, that they should build the lexicons, gazetteers and structured data that software developers will not, is the premise of the exercise.

Why this example

The dispute is small enough to read in the hour and rich enough to contain every kind of decision the workflow needs:

  • a person with a strong identity (Trimen) and people with none in any authority file (Agar, Christie);
  • a relationship the source states carefully and the journal’s own editor states loosely;
  • plant names that a modern authority treats differently from the 1883 participants;
  • an OCR error that reverses a sentence’s meaning.
