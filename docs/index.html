One dataset, start to finish

Two printed reference works, the Colonial Office List (1862–1966) and the India Office List (1886–1947), carry a terse biography for every senior official: born, schooled, every posting, every honour. The same man recurs in forty annual editions. The OCR garbles a name here and there. Two men who share a surname threaten to collapse into one career.

Over a few months, one historian with a coding agent turned those volumes into a knowledge graph and an animated atlas of imperial careers:

Colonial Office List India Office List
People 27,526 19,400
Dated career events 180,470 124,694
Events located to a Wikidata place 131,343 80,238

The hour is about how, and about where in that process a historian has to decide something.

Three questions

Technical terms are explained where they first appear on each page, and in the glossary.

A historian turning a text into data asks three questions of it, and each has one decision only the historian can make. A model can do everything around that decision, at scale.

The question The model does You decide
Structure What is a record here? Proposes the fields, fills them for 100,000 entries, reports what it could not parse What counts as an event; whether the record changed the meaning
Clean Is this one person or two? Clusters the candidates, argues each case, keeps the evidence Merge, keep apart, or leave for review
Ground Which place, which person, in the shared vocabulary? Retrieves candidates with evidence Supported, unresolved, or wrong

The second half of the method

Extraction is half of it. The other half is looking at the result in a form where errors are visible. The atlas was built to answer a research question, and it turned out to be the best error detector the project had. A British Columbia premier appearing in Australia, a cluster of South African postings landing at Harare, two nodes both called Baluchistan: each was seen on the map, traced to a rule, fixed in the pipeline, and re-emitted for everyone it affected. Build, extract, visualise, see, fix, rerun. That loop is what the hour demonstrates.

What you need

A browser. The decision exercise and the mark-up exercise run from saved results and need no account or key. Remote participants have the same pages; each exercise has a button that copies a one-line summary of your work for the Zoom chat. There is a printable handout with the same entries for the room.
